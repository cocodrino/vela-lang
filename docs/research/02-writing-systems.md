## 1. Introduction: Scripts as Cultural Artifacts

A writing system is far more than a set of symbols — it encodes cultural values, historical forces, technological constraints, and aesthetic philosophies. When designing a script for VELA, it's essential to understand how real scripts emerged, evolved, and what makes them feel ancient vs. modern, sacred vs. secular, complex vs. simple.

This document examines:
1. How writing systems are classified
2. Case studies of invented/modern scripts: Cherokee, Hangul, Zhuyin
3. Principles that distinguish ancient from modern scripts
4. How scripts evolve over time
5. Practical recommendations for VELA's writing system

## 2. Classification of Writing Systems

### 2.1 Primary Types

**Logographic:** Each symbol represents a morpheme/word. Chinese (Hanzi), Egyptian hieroglyphics, Mayan glyphs. Very large inventories (thousands of symbols), but many are composed of phonetic and semantic components.

**Syllabic:** Each symbol represents a syllable. Cherokee (85 characters), Cypriot syllabary, Linear B. More economical than logograms but still requires many symbols for complex phonologies.

**Alphabetic (Abjad, Abugida, Alphabet):**
- **Abjad:** Only consonants are written; vowels omitted or indicated optionally (Arabic, Hebrew). Called "impure alphabets."
- **Abugida:** Consonants carry an inherent vowel; vowel modification shown with diacritics (Devanagari, Thai, Ethiopic). Most scripts in South and Southeast Asia.
- **Alphabet:** Both consonants and vowels have equal-status letters (Latin, Cyrillic, Greek, Hangul).

**Featural:** The letters encode distinctive phonetic features (place, manner, voicing). **Hangul** is the most famous and only widely-used featural script in modern times.

### 2.2 Mixed and Hybrid Systems

Most scripts are not purely one type. Japanese uses:
- Kanji (logographic Chinese characters for morphemes)
- Hiragana (syllabic, derived from Chinese cursive)
- Katakana (syllabic, derived from Chinese components)
- Romaji (Latin alphabet for foreign words)

This is called a **parallel system** — multiple writing systems serving different functions in the same language.

## 3. Case Study: Cherokee Syllabary ( invented 1820s)

### 3.1 Historical Context

Cherokee is one of the most remarkable cases of script invention in history. **Sequoyah** (c. 1770–c. 1840), a Cherokee blacksmith with no literacy in any language, devised the syllabary between 1809 and 1824. The script was officially adopted by the Cherokee Nation in 1825 and spread with extraordinary speed — within months, thousands of Cherokees achieved literacy.

### 3.2 Design Principles

**The Syllabic Principle:**
Each of the 85 characters represents a syllable, not an individual sound. This is a syllabary, not an alphabet. The structure reflects Cherokee's syllable patterns:
- Syllables are primarily CV (consonant + vowel)
- Vowels can also stand alone as syllables

**Character Organization:**
The modern arrangement (standardized by missionary Samuel Worcester) organizes characters by:
1. Six vowel-only syllables (a, e, i, o, u, v — Cherokee has six vowels)
2. Consonant+vowel combinations organized by consonant, with rows for each vowel

**Visual Resemblance = Coincidence:**
Some Cherokee characters visually resemble Latin, Greek, or Cyrillic letters, but this is coincidental — Sequoyah was influenced by the shapes he saw in English printed material, tracing them as abstract shapes without understanding their sounds. Each Cherokee character represents a completely different Cherokee sound.

**Principles for VELA:**
- The Cherokee syllabary shows that an invented script can achieve rapid literacy when it aligns with the language's natural phonological structure
- A script need not look like an existing script to work — it can draw from visual elements while having independent sound-symbol mapping
- **Rapid adoption** is possible when the script reflects the language's structure efficiently

### 3.3 The Cherokee Writing Revival

In the 21st century, Cherokee has undergone a written-language revival. The syllabary is used on street signs, government documents, and in education. The Cherokee Phoenix newspaper, established in 1828, was the first bilingual newspaper in the US.

## 4. Case Study: Hangul (1446)

### 4.1 Historical Context

King Sejong the Great promulgated **Hangul** in 1446 with the publication of the *Hunminjeongeum* ("The Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People"). It was specifically designed to replace or supplement the use of Chinese characters (Hanja) among common people, who struggled with the years of study required to achieve literacy in the classical Chinese writing system.

### 4.2 Design Principles

**Featural Organization:**
Hangul is unique in that its **letter shapes encode the articulatory geometry** of the sounds they represent:

| Letter | Shape Represents |
|--------|-----------------|
| ㄱ /g/ | Back of tongue blocking throat (velar) |
| ㄴ /n/ | Tongue touching upper gums (alveolar) |
| ㅁ /m/ | Shape of closed lips |
| ㅅ /s/ | Shape of teeth |
| ㅇ /ŋ/ or silent | Shape of throat |

**Stroke Addition System:**
From the five basic consonants, others are derived systematically by adding strokes:
- ㄱ → ㅋ (aspirated k): added stroke = more air
- ㄱ → ㄲ (tense g): double stroke = tense/glottal constriction
- ㄴ → ㄷ → ㅌ (aspirated t): same pattern
- ㄴ → ㄹ (lateral liquid r/l): modified shape

**Vowel Organization:**
Vowels are composed from three elements representing philosophical principles:
- 'ㅡ' (horizontal line) = earth
- 'ㅣ' (vertical line) = human standing
- 'ㅗ' / 'ㅏ' = combination principles

Other vowels are formed by combining these: 'ㅜ' combines 'ㅡ' and 'ㅗ', etc.

**Syllabic Block Structure:**
Letters are arranged into syllabic blocks, typically in a 2x2 or 3x2 grid:
- Initial consonant (chō) + vowel (yoon) + optional final consonant (jongsung)
- Read left-to-right and top-to-bottom within the block

### 4.3 The Science of Hangul

Scholars have demonstrated that Hangul is remarkably efficient:
- Only 24 basic letters (14 consonants + 10 vowels originally, now 14 + 10)
- Can represent all Korean syllables with a finite set of rules
- King Sejong claimed: "A wise man may learn them before the morning is over; even a foolish man in ten days"

**Anti-Chinese Motivation:** Hangul was revolutionary because it was explicitly designed for accessibility and democratic literacy — not as a sacred or scholarly system, but as a practical tool for ordinary people.

**Principles for VELA:**
- Featural scripts (where letter shapes encode phonetic features) are extremely rare — Hangul is the only successful modern example
- Even if VELA doesn't use a fully featural script, elements of featural design can add visual coherence and logical elegance
- The most successful invented scripts prioritize accessibility and alignment with the language's structure

## 5. Case Study: Zhuyin (1900s)

### 5.1 Historical Context

**Zhuyin Fuhao** ("phonetic symbols for sounds") was developed in Taiwan in 1906-1908 and officially adopted by the Republic of China in 1918 as the official phonetic system for teaching Mandarin Chinese. It was designed as a stepping-stone to literacy, helping students learn the pronunciation of Chinese characters before learning the characters themselves.

### 5.2 Design Principles

**Syllabic Organization:**
Like Cherokee, Zhuyin uses syllabic symbols, but with a different organizational principle:
- 37 initials (consonants) + 16 finals (vowels and glides)
- Combinations of initials + finals produce all possible syllables

**Visual Symbol Shapes:**
Zhuyin symbols were designed to be visually distinct, compact, and easy to write quickly. Many symbols are derived from classical Chinese radicals and components, lending them cultural familiarity while serving a purely phonetic function.

**Tone Markers:**
Zhuyin includes tone marks — small symbols indicating the four tones of Mandarin — attached to the syllable. This is notable because Chinese characters don't indicate tone, making Zhuyin a more complete representation of spoken Chinese.

**Decline and Modern Use:**
Zhuyin was largely supplanted by Pinyin (Latin alphabet romanization) in Mainland China after 1958, but remains widely used in Taiwan for educational purposes and in input method editors. Zhuyin's advantage: it's faster to write than Pinyin for native speakers.

**Principles for VELA:**
- Zhuyin shows that romanization systems can serve as bridges to a native script — useful if VELA is ever presented in both phonetic and traditional orthography
- The inclusion of tone markers in the script reflects the language's phonological structure (tone)
- Scripts don't need to replace existing systems immediately; they can coexist as complementary tools

## 6. Ancient vs. Modern Scripts: What Creates the Distinction?

### 6.1 Visual Complexity

**Ancient/Classical scripts** tend to have:
- More complex, curved, or intricate strokes
- Calligraphic variation (cursive vs. inscriptional forms)
- Logographic or syllabic systems with large inventories
- Visual decoration and aesthetic flourishes integrated with letters

**Modern scripts** tend to have:
- Simplified, straight-line forms (Latin sans-serif, Hangul, machine-printed Arabic)
- Reduced stroke count per character
- Alphabetic or abugida systems (fewer distinct symbols)
- Clean, utilitarian aesthetic

**The Industrial Revolution's Influence:**
The printing press, typewriter, and computer keyboard have dramatically simplified all scripts. Even Chinese characters have been standardized into simplified forms in Mainland China. Cherokee syllabary, designed in the 1820s, has relatively simple, printed forms — it never developed a complex calligraphy tradition.

### 6.2 Directionality

Scripts vary in writing direction:
- **Left-to-right:** Latin, Cyrillic, Hangul, modern Arabic
- **Right-to-left:** Arabic, Hebrew, Syriac
- **Boustrophedon:** Alternating direction (ancient Greek inscriptions, some early Semitic scripts)
- **Vertical top-to-bottom:** Traditional Chinese, Mongolian, old Uyghur, Japanese columns

Writing direction profoundly affects layout, book design, and the feel of a script. Vertical writing creates columns read top-to-bottom, which is the traditional Chinese/Japanese mode.

### 6.3 Calligraphic vs. Inscriptional Forms

Nearly all ancient scripts have at least two registers:
1. **Inscriptional/Carved:** Designed for stone, metal, or clay — bold, simplified strokes
2. **Cursive/Manuscript:** Designed for reed pens, brushes, ink — flowing, connected forms

Examples:
- Hieroglyphics (carved) vs. Hieratic (cursive Egyptian)
- Classical Latin (inscribed) vs. Medieval uncials (manuscript)
- Brāhmī (inscribed) vs. Devanāgarī (developed cursive)
- Arabic Kufic (early, angular) vs. Naskh (flowing cursive)

**VELA Recommendation:** Design at least two forms: a formal/inscriptional style (for titles, documents) and a cursive/manuscript style (for everyday writing). This distinction adds historical depth and plausibility.

## 7. How Scripts Evolve Over Time

### 7.1 Stages of Script Evolution

Scripts never remain static. They undergo predictable changes:

**Stage 1 — Pictographic Origins:**
Most scripts begin as pictures representing objects. Egyptian hieroglyphics, Sumerian cuneiform, and early Chinese characters all began this way.

**Stage 2 — Semantic Generalization:**
Pictures become abstract symbols representing broader concepts. The original pictographic meaning becomes opaque.

**Stage 3 — Phoneticization:**
Logograms acquire phonetic values. Chinese "假" (jiǎ) originally meaning "false/borrow" came to also represent the sound /jiǎ/ in phonetic borrowings. This is how true alphabets eventually emerged independently in Phoenician, Mayan, and Hangul.

**Stage 4 — Alphabet Extraction:**
Phoenician letters (c. 1050 BCE) were purely consonantal, derived from Egyptian hieroglyphs by taking the first sound of each word (the acrophonic principle: 'Aleph' = 'ox' = /ʔ/).

**Stage 5 — Vowel Addition:**
Greek (c. 800 BCE) added vowels to the Phoenician consonantal system — the first "true" alphabet. This innovation spread to Latin and ultimately to most of the modern world.

### 7.2 Sound Change and Script

As spoken languages change, writing systems often lag behind:

- English spelling retains sounds from Middle English (knight, write, psychology)
- Arabic spelling reflects Classical Arabic pronunciation, not modern colloquial
- Italian orthography captures 16th-century pronunciation more faithfully than modern speech
- Sanskrit written in Devanagari preserves sounds from millennia ago

This **conservatism** creates a disconnect between writing and speech — the script encodes an older form of the language. This is a natural, universal process.

### 7.3 Script Reform and Simplification

Languages periodically reform their scripts:
- Turkey switched from Arabic script to Latin letters in 1928 under Atatürk
- Mainland China simplified characters in the 1950s-60s; Taiwan/Hong Kong retained traditional forms
- Vietnamese abandoned Chữ Nôm (Chinese characters adapted for Vietnamese) for Quốc Ngữ (Latin script) in the 17th century, fully adopted by 20th century
- Korean Hanja (Chinese characters) remain in limited use alongside Hangul

## 8. Designing a VELA Script

### 8.1 What Type of Script?

For VELA, a featural or alphabetic script would be most naturalistic. Options:

**Option A — Alphabet (recommended):**
- 17-20 distinct symbols for consonants + 5 for vowels
- Roman-letter-inspired or entirely original shapes
- Simple phonemic orthography (1 letter = 1 phoneme mostly)

**Option B — Abugida:**
- Consonants carry inherent vowel, diacritics modify
- More complex but visually distinctive
- Good if VELA has a rich consonant inventory with few vowels

**Option C — Featural Alphabet:**
- Hangul-inspired letter shapes encoding articulatory features
- Extremely rare and distinctive
- High creative investment but highly original

### 8.2 Recommended Design Principles

1. **Limit stroke types:** Use no more than 4-5 basic stroke shapes (straight line, curve, circle, dot, hook). This keeps the script learnable and visually coherent.
2. **Use curves for vowels, straight lines for consonants** (or vice versa) — this is attested in many scripts and creates immediate visual parsing.
3. **Design for left-to-right horizontal writing** — most modern scripts use this; it's the current global standard.
4. **Add diacritics sparingly** — only for tone, vowel length, stress, or nasalization.
5. **Include numeral forms** — custom numerals add cultural completeness.
6. **Create both majuscule (uppercase) and minuscule (lowercase) forms** if using an alphabetic script — this adds historical depth and visual range.

### 8.3 Historical Development Scenario

Write a backstory for VELA's script:
- Originally a pictographic system (50-100 symbols for objects)
- Evolved to syllabic (representing the most common CV combinations)
- Further abstracted to alphabetic
- Calligraphic variants developed by different cultural centers
- Orthographic reforms at key historical moments standardized the system

This backstory can be documented as part of VELA's conhistory and adds depth to the language.

### 8.4 Sample Script Elements (Conceptual)

| Element | Design |
|---------|--------|
| Consonants | Angular, straight-line strokes |
| Vowels | Curved, flowing strokes |
| Tone marks | Small diacritics above/below the syllable |
| Numerals | Vertical strokes with dots (base-5 influenced) |
| Punctuation | Vertical dots for sentence ends, horizontal dash for pause |

## 9. Ancient vs. Modern Feeling: Practical Checklist

To make a VELA script feel **ancient/historical**:
- [ ] Use curved, calligraphic stroke shapes
- [ ] Develop a cursive variant with connected letters
- [ ] Add decorative flourishes to letter terminals
- [ ] Use a mix of tall/short letters (ascenders/descenders)
- [ ] Include letterform variants (allographs) for the same sound
- [ ] Develop complex ligatures (two letters merged)
- [ ] Write a history of orthographic reforms and variants
- [ ] Include archaic letterforms alongside modern ones

To make a VELA script feel **modern/efficient**:
- [ ] Use geometric, simplified letter shapes
- [ ] Minimize stroke count per character
- [ ] Design for digital readability (clear at small sizes)
- [ ] Use a consistent baseline and x-height
- [ ] Avoid complex ligatures and allographs
- [ ] Make the writing direction clear with minimal punctuation
