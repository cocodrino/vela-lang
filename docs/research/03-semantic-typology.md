## Research Document 3 — Non-Anglocentric Categorization of Reality

## 1. Introduction: The Problem with Anglocentrism

Natural languages do not merely encode the same universal concepts in different sounds. They carve up reality differently — sometimes radically so. A language may have no word for "time" as an abstract concept (and therefore no grammatical tenses), no color terms for blue vs. green (a single "grue" category), or no distinction between "give" and "receive" at all.

**Anglocentrism** in conlang design occurs when a creator unconsciously maps English semantic categories onto the conlang, producing a language that feels like English in fancy dress. Building a truly non-Anglocentric conlang requires understanding how different languages conceptualize:

- Color
- Space and direction
- Time
- Objects and classification
- Events and processes
- Knowledge and evidence (evidentiality)
- Social relations and respect

This document surveys how natural languages categorize these domains and provides guidance for VELA's semantic design.

## 2. Color Terms: Berlin & Kay and Beyond

### 2.1 The Berlin & Kay Theory (1969)

Brent Berlin and Paul Kay's research on basic color terms established one of the most famous hierarchies in linguistic typology. They found that:

**All languages draw from the same 11 basic color categories:**
:white_circle: **WHITE/BLACK** (light/dark or bright/warm vs. dark/cool)
:red_circle: **RED**
:yellow_circle: **YELLOW**
:green_circle: **GREEN**
:blue_circle: **BLUE**
:brown_circle: **BROWN**
:purple_circle: **PURPLE**
:pink_circle: **PINK**
:orange_circle: **ORANGE**
:gray_circle: **GRAY**

**The Implicational Hierarchy:**
```
black + white → red → yellow OR green → green + yellow → blue → brown → 
purple + pink + orange + gray
```

**What this means:** If a language has 3 basic color terms, they ARE black, white, and red. A language with 4 terms adds either yellow OR green. No natural language has blue without also having green, yellow, red, black, and white.

### 2.2 Critiques and Refinements

The original theory was revised after the **World Color Survey** (WCS, 1970s-2000s) collected data from 110 unwritten languages:

1. **Color foci are universal:** Despite variation in color boundaries, the "best examples" of each color are remarkably stable across cultures (red = red, not a shifted red).

2. **Color boundaries vary:** Languages draw boundaries between categories differently. Russian divides blue into *goluboy* (light blue) and *siniy* (dark blue) — two basic terms where English has one.

3. **The hierarchy is probabilistic, not deterministic:** Some languages deviate from the strict ordering. There are a few documented exceptions to the implicational pattern.

4. **"Grue" and "Ble":** Some languages have a single term covering what English calls green+blue (Ross 2006 documents several). This is possible but uncommon.

### 2.3 Beyond Basic Color Terms

**Secondary color terms** vary enormously across cultures:
- **Russian:** *gery* (gray), *rozhiy* (pink), *korichneviy* (brown)
- **Greek:** *galanos* (pale blue) as a distinct concept
- **Japanese:** *midori* (green), *ao* (blue/green) — the boundary differs from English
- **Korean:** *cheong* (blue/green) vs. Japanese different division
- **Hausa:** *fat* (white/black in brightness sense), extensive color vocabulary from dye trade culture

**Cultural salience:** What colors are culturally significant shapes vocabulary. Many languages have extensive terms for cattle coat colors, textile dyes, or skin tones that don't translate into English color terms.

**VELA Recommendation:** VELA should follow the Berlin & Kay hierarchy but with a specific cultural aesthetic:
- If VELA is a nature-oriented culture, it might have very precise botanical green terms at the expense of some other terms
- If it's an ocean/coastal culture, it might divide blue-green more precisely than European languages
- At minimum, VELA needs: white/dark, red, and one of yellow/green at Stage III
- Consider whether warm/cool is more basic than white/black in VELA's conceptual system

## 3. Spatial Metaphors and Directional Systems

### 3.1 The Universality of Spatial Metaphors

All languages use spatial metaphors to describe abstract concepts, but the specific mappings vary. The concept **TIME** is almost universally metaphorized as **SPACE** — but which spatial dimension maps to time differs:

- **EGO-REFERENCE POINT:** Most languages use the speaker's position as the reference point for time ("next week" = the week ahead of NOW).
- **FRONT = FUTURE, BACK = PAST:** English, German, Spanish, Japanese — the body faces forward, the past is behind
- **LEFT = PAST, RIGHT = FUTURE:** Some languages in Papua New Guinea
- **UP = PAST, DOWN = FUTURE:** Aymara (Bolivia) — the past is in front (visible) and the future behind (unseen)

### 3.2 Cardinal vs. Relative Direction Systems

Languages differ in how they encode spatial directions:

**Relative systems** (English, most Indo-European): Use intrinsic directions (left/right) or deictic directions (here/there, this/that).

**Cardinal systems** (most indigenous Australian languages, Tzeltal, Guugu Yimithirr): Use cardinal directions (north/south/east/west) regardless of the speaker's position. Speakers of these languages must always know absolute orientation — they say things like "there's a fly on your northwest arm."

**Geographical systems** (Japanese): Use a mix — relative for near objects, cardinal/directional for larger scales.

**VELA Recommendation:** VELA could use:
1. A **cardinal direction system** for abstract/spatial metaphors (time, social hierarchy)
2. A **relative system** for physical manipulation (on the table)
This is cognitively complex but typologically attested (Japanese).

### 3.3 The Spatial Basis of Grammatical Categories

Many grammatical features have spatial origins:
- **Tense:** From Latin *tempus* (time), but many languages grammaticalize tense from spatial concepts
- **Case:** Often derived from spatial postpositions (Latin *-um* in *romam* "to Rome" from direction *-om*)
- **Aspect:** Progressive/continuous forms often come from spatial proximity or posture verbs ("be at/in" → ongoing)

## 4. Tense-Aspect Systems

### 4.1 Tense vs. Aspect: The Fundamental Distinction

**Tense:** Location of an event in time (past, present, future). Grammaticalizes *when*.

**Aspect:** How an event is experienced or conceptualized (ongoing, completed, habitual, instantaneous). Grammaticalizes *how*.

Most languages grammaticalize at least one of these, but the specific choices vary enormously.

### 4.2 Aspect Systems: Major Patterns

**Perfective vs. Imperfective:**
- **Perfective:** Views the event as a complete whole ("Yesterday I went to the store")
- **Imperfective:** Views the event from inside, as ongoing or habitual ("I was going to the store when..." or "I go to the store every day")

This is the most common aspectual distinction, found in Slavic languages (Russian, Polish), Romance languages, Arabic, and many others.

**Progressive/Continuous:**
- English: "I am reading" vs. "I read"
- Yoruba, Igbo, and many West African languages have obligatory progressive marking
- Not universal — Japanese lacks a grammatical progressive; Chinese uses context

**Habitual:**
- Distinguishes "I smoke" (as a habit) from "I smoked" (once)
- Australian languages often have habitual past, habitual present, etc.

**Completive/Perfect:**
- "I have finished" (resultant state)
- English Present Perfect has no equivalent in Russian, Chinese, or Japanese

### 4.3 Tense Systems: Major Patterns

**Two-way tense** (past/non-past): English, Chinese, Japanese
- English: past vs. non-past (present-future combined)
- Mandarin has no grammatical tense; time indicated by aspect markers and adverbs

**Three-way tense** (past/present/future): Spanish, Russian, German
- Russian actually has a two-way past/non-past system plus aspect complexity

**No grammatical tense:** Chinese, Vietnamese, Malay — time is expressed lexically and aspectually

**Relative tense:** Some languages mark tense relative to the reference time, not absolute time (Mandarin distant vs. near past)

### 4.4 Evidentiality: Encoding the Source of Knowledge

Evidentiality is a grammatical category that encodes how the speaker knows what they're saying:

**Direct evidence:**
- Visual: "I saw it happen"
- Auditory: "I heard it"
- Sensory (other): "I smelled it burning"

**Indirect evidence:**
- Inferential: "It must have rained" (I see evidence)
- Quotative/Hearsay: "They say he's left" (I heard from someone)

**Languages with evidentiality:**
- **Quechua:** Obligatory suffix system with visual, non-visual, inferred, reported
- **Aymara:** Similar system with strong visual/non-visual distinction
- **Korean:** -(i)da (direct experience) vs. -(i)ram (indirect/inferential) — though debated as true evidentiality
- **Japanese:** Limited evidential marking with sentence-final particles
- **Turkish:** -mış (hearsay/inferential) vs. -di (direct) past tense
- **Warlpiri** (Australia): Evidentials obligatory

**Geographic distribution:** Evidentiality is prevalent in the Americas, the Caucasus, the Himalayan region, and Papua New Guinea. It is largely absent in European and African languages.

**VELA Recommendation:** VELA could include a simple evidentiality system:
- **Direct/non-visual** distinction (I saw it vs. I heard/deduced it)
- **Reported** (I was told)
- This adds enormous semantic richness for very little grammatical cost

### 4.5 The Resultant State (Perfect) Challenge

English's Present Perfect ("I have finished") is notoriously difficult for learners because it's a blend of past tense and resultant state. Many languages solve this differently:

- **German:** Perfekt uses auxiliary + past participle
- **Russian:** Perfective aspect handles this (I have written = I wrote, completed)
- **French:** Passé composé (similar to German)
- **Japanese:** Context; -te-iru form
- **Turkish:** -mış for distant past/hearsay

VELA could avoid the English Perfect problem entirely by using aspectual distinctions rather than temporal ones.

## 5. Classifier Systems

### 5.1 What Are Classifiers?

Classifiers are morphemes (words or affixes) that categorize nouns based on semantic properties when they occur with numerals, demonstratives, or verbs.

**Numeral Classifiers** (most common type):
- In a classifier language: "three CL(tree) tall" = "three meters tall"
- English has lost this but retains some traces: "three head of cattle," "two pieces of paper"

**Classifier languages include:** Chinese (extensive), Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai, Tibetan, Mayan languages, many Australian Aboriginal languages, Mixe-Zoque languages.

### 5.2 Classifier Categories

Classifiers categorize nouns by:
- **Shape:** long, round, flat
- **Size:** small, large
- **Consistency:** solid, liquid, flexible
- **Function:** edible, drinkable, countable
- **Material:** cloth, metal, wood
- **Location:** things in vertical orientation, horizontal, etc.

**Chinese example:**
- 张 (zhāng): flat, extended objects (paper, tables, mouths)
- 条 (tiáo): long, thin, flexible (rope, fish, rivers)
- 棵 (kē): trees and plants
- 只 (zhī): animals, some utensils

### 5.3 Noun Class and Gender Systems

A more grammaticalized version of classification is **noun classes** (gender systems), found in:
- **Indo-European:** typically 2-3 genders (French 2, German 3, Russian 3)
- **Bantu languages:** 10-20 noun classes (Swahili has 15)
- **Dravidian languages:** 2 genders (masculine/feminine + neuter)
- **Semitic:** 2 genders with grammatical agreement

**VELA Recommendation:** A classifier system is complex to implement grammatically but adds great semantic richness. A simpler alternative is a **noun class system** (like Bantu or Indo-European) with 3-5 classes marked on adjectives and verbs. Classes could be:
1. Human/sapient
2. Animate/non-human
3. Liquid/mass
4. Objects/tools
5. Abstract/uncountable

## 6. The Categorization of Events: Verb Semantics

### 6.1 Motion and Path

Languages differ dramatically in how they encode motion:

**Satellite-framed languages** (English, German, Russian): The path of motion is expressed in a satellite (preposition, particle) while the manner is in the verb:
- "walked OUT of the room"
- "ran INTO the building"

**Verb-framed languages** (Spanish, Turkish, Japanese): The path is encoded in the verb while manner is expressed separately:
- Spanish: *salió corriendo* (left-RUNNING) = ran out
- The path is part of the verb root (*salir* = to exit)

** equipollently-framed languages** (Chinese, Tamil): Both manner and path are encoded at the same grammatical level.

**VELA Recommendation:** Choose one framing type and be consistent. Verb-framed languages tend to have more verb roots related to motion direction (useful if VELA needs many path verbs). Satellite-framed languages allow more manner-verb creativity.

### 6.2 "Give" and "Take" Asymmetries

English conflates direction in "give" and "send":
- "Send me the book" = I receive it
- "Send you the book" = you receive it

Many languages distinguish **benefactive/harmful** and **proximity to speaker**:
- Japanese: あげる (age-ru = give up), くれる (kure-ru = give to me), もらう (morau = receive)
- Mandarin: 给 (gěi) is neutral; directional serial verb constructions add specificity
- Korean: 주다 (juda = give) with social hierarchy markers

**Existential, Possessive, and Being:**

Many languages use the same root for "be at a location," "have," and "exist":
- Japanese: ある (aru) for existence of things, いる (iru) for animate existence, 持つ (motsu) for possession
- Russian uses быть (byt') for existence, but possessive constructions differ
- Finnish: on = "is/are/exists" and "has" (olla = to be; minulla on = "to me is" = I have)

VELA could fuse these concepts or keep them separate — both are naturalistic choices.

## 7. Non-Anglocentric Semantic Design for VELA

### 7.1 Key Decisions Matrix

| Domain | Anglocentric Default | Non-Anglocentric Alternative | VELA Choice |
|--------|---------------------|------------------------------|-------------|
| Color terms | English 11-term system | Follow B&K hierarchy with cultural specifics | Follow B&K, add 2 cultural tertiary terms |
| Time metaphor | Front=future | Front=future OR Aymara-style front=past | Front=future (most common) |
| Spatial directions | Relative (left/right) | Cardinal (north/south) | Mixed: relative for objects, cardinal for large-scale |
| Tense | Past/Present/Future | Optional: Present/Past only (aspect handles future) | Present/Past + Future-as-modality |
| Aspect | Progressive/Perfect | Two-way perfective/imperfective | Perfective/Imperfective + Habitual |
| Evidentiality | None | Direct/Inferential/Reported | Direct/Hearsay/Inferential |
| Classifiers | None | Noun classes OR numeral classifiers | 4 noun classes (sapient, animate, mass, object) |
| "Have" | Dedicated possessive verb | "to-me-is" construction OR existential | Existential + possessive construction |
| Motion | Satellite-framed | Verb-framed | Verb-framed |
| "Know" | Single verb | "know-that" / "know-how" / "be-acquainted" | Distinguish factual vs. familiar knowledge |

### 7.2 Avoiding Anglocentrism: Practical Tips

1. **Don't assume abstract "time" exists as a grammatical category** — many languages don't grammaticalize it
2. **Don't assume "give" and "receive" are symmetric** — many languages use the same root or constructions
3. **Don't assume "have" is universal** — existential constructions are equally valid
4. **Don't assume subject-verb-object is universal** — other orders exist
5. **Don't assume the self is at the center of spatial metaphors** — cardinal systems put the world at the center
6. **Don't assume emotion words are the same** — some languages have more granularity in emotion categories
7. **Check translations** — go back and forth between English and VELA, looking for hidden assumptions

### 7.3 Creating Semantic Gaps and Untranslatables

Part of what makes a conlang feel foreign and interesting is having concepts that don't map cleanly to English:

- **Japanese:** *木漏れ日* (komorebi) = the light that filters through leaves — no English single word
- **German:** *Schadenfreude* = pleasure from others' misfortune
- **Korean:** *han* = the pain of a single day; *woe* but also loneliness
- **Russian:** *tocka* = a precise moment; precise spatial/temporal point
- **Inuktitut:** Many terms for different types of ice, snow, and sea conditions
- **Guugu Yimithirr:** Cardinal directions at all times — no "left" or "right"

VELA should have its own **untranslatables** — concepts that reveal its unique cultural and ecological perspective.
