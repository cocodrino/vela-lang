## 1. Introduction

Constructed languages have existed for centuries, but the modern conlanging movement has produced a remarkable diversity of languages with vastly different philosophies, goals, and designs. Studying existing conlangs — both successful and experimental — provides invaluable guidance for VELA.

This document examines seven influential constructed languages across the full spectrum of conlang purposes, extracting design lessons and noting what to emulate or avoid.

## 2. Esperanto (1887)

### 2.1 Background

**Creator:** L. L. Zamenhof (Polish ophthalmologist, 1859–1917)
**Type:** International auxiliary language (IAL)
**Goal:** Facilitate communication across linguistic divides without replacing national languages
**Date:** Published 1887 (*Unua Libro*); revised in 1905 (*Fundamento de Esperanto*)

### 2.2 Design Philosophy

Zamenhof's guiding principle was **regularity and transparency**: every morphological rule should apply without exception, every word should be decomposable into analyzable parts, and the grammar should be learnable in weeks, not years.

**Key Design Features:**

1. **Phonological simplicity:** 5 vowels (a, e, i, o, u), one consonant per letter, no digraphs, every sound spelled unambiguously
2. **Agglutinative morphology:** Prefix + root + suffix + ending (e.g., *bon-o* = good (noun), *bon-a* = good (adj.), *bon-e* = well (adv.); *el-* = out, *-et-* = diminutive, *-ul-* = person → *bonetulo* = a little good person)
3. **Correlative system:** Systematic table of demonstratives, interrogatives, and quantifiers:
   - *kiu* (who/which), *tiu* (that one), *ĉiu* (every one), *neniu* (no one), *iu* (someone)
4. **No grammatical gender** (except in pronouns with sex reference)
5. **Accusative case (-n) for direct objects** — simple, regular
6. **16 rules, no exceptions** — the famous regularity promise

### 2.3 What Works

- **Transparency:** Esperanto words are genuinely decomposable. A learner can guess the meaning of unknown words from their parts.
- **Aesthetic consistency:** The -o ending for nouns, -a for adjectives, -e for adverbs, -i for infinitives gives a pleasing rhythmic regularity.
- **International vocabulary:** Roots drawn from Romance, Germanic, and Slavic languages make many words recognizable to European speakers.
- **Living community:** ~2 million speakers, active online community, original literature, translation tradition.

### 2.4 What Doesn't Work (Criticisms)

- **Eurocentrism despite claims of neutrality:** The phonology, vocabulary, and grammar are unmistakably European. Asian speakers find it much harder than European speakers.
- **Accusative awkwardness:** The -n ending for direct objects is a Latin/Germanic feature that many find unnecessary.
- **Free word order betrayed by accusative:** While Zamenhof intended free word order, in practice word order is fairly rigid because the accusative marking is cumbersome.
- **False friends in abundance:** Despite attempts at internationalism, Esperanto often uses words from unexpected sources.

### 2.5 Lessons for VELA

- **Regularity is not optional** — it's the foundation of learnability and naturalistic feel
- **But regularity shouldn't come at the cost of all naturalness** — Zamenhof's perfect regularity sometimes makes Esperanto feel sterile
- **International vocabulary is a double-edged sword** — recognizable words are easy, but they import Eurocentric biases
- **A correlative system** (like Esperanto's *kiu-tiu-ĉiu-neniu* table) adds elegant systematicity to a conlang
- **VELA should balance regularity with organic irregularity** — perhaps dialectal variation or historical sound changes add naturalness without sacrificing usability

## 3. Klingon (1984)

### 3.1 Background

**Creator:** Marc Okrand (American linguist)
**Type:** Fictional auxiliary language for the Klingon species in Star Trek
**Goal:** Sound alien and militaristic, support Klingon cultural worldbuilding
**Date:** Created 1984 for *Star Trek III: The Search for Spock*; expanded in subsequent films and TV series

### 3.2 Design Philosophy

Okrand's goal was to create a language that **sounded harsh, guttural, and military** — reflecting the Klingon warrior culture. The language was specifically designed to be pronounceable by human actors while maintaining its alien character.

**Key Design Features:**

1. **Unusual phonology:** 
   - Ejectives (*p', t', q'*) — produced with a glottal stop, creating a sharp, explosive quality
   - No /b/, /d/, /g/ as separate phonemes (replaced by /p/, /t/, /k/ with different voicing characteristics)
   - Voiceless lateral fricative /x/ and uvular stop /q/ — sounds absent from English
   - Example: *nuqneH* "What do you want?" (famous line)
   
2. **Verb-final word order:** SOV (subject-object-verb) — head-final, typical of many East Asian and Native American languages

3. **No gender-based agreement** — pronouns are neutral

4. **Prefix system** indicating person/number of subject and object simultaneously:
   - *jIH* = I; *qaH* = he/it
   - *qaH vIlob* = "I see him/it" (3rd-singular object prefix *vI-* + 1st-singular subject prefix *jI-* collapses to *vI-*)
   
5. **Compound-heavy vocabulary** with cultural focus:
   - *Qapla'* = success (used for congratulations)
   - *nuqneH* = what do you want?
   - *petaQ* = sneak (derogatory for Cardassians)

### 3.3 What Works

- **Distinctive sound:** The ejectives and uvulars give Klingon an unmistakable aggressive quality
- **Cultural coherence:** Vocabulary and grammar reflect a warrior culture — honor, combat, loyalty are prominent
- **Real linguistic depth:** Despite being a fictional language, Klingon has productive morphology, derivational systems, and even a growing body of original literature (translated Shakespeare, etc.)
- **Living community:** The Klingon Language Institute (1992-1994) and online communities have produced substantial linguistic analysis and creative usage

### 3.4 What Doesn't Work (Criticisms)

- **Designed primarily for aesthetics, not learnability:** Klingon is hard to learn, deliberately so — Okrand didn't want it to be easy
- **Very small vocabulary:** Official vocabulary is only ~2,000 words (versus 10,000+ for Esperanto)
- **Limited morphology:** Despite having some affixation, Klingon feels constrained compared to natural polysynthetic languages
- **Designed for English speakers first:** The phonology was designed around what English-speaking actors could pronounce, not what would be natural for the Klingon species

### 3.5 Lessons for VELA

- **Sound should reflect culture:** Klingon's harsh, military sound perfectly serves its cultural context. VELA's phonology should reflect its speakers' worldview and environment.
- **Vocabulary should have cultural bias:** Klingon's cultural vocabulary (honor, combat, duty) is extensive; abstract vocabulary is sparse. VELA should invest in vocabulary for concepts that matter to its culture.
- **But don't let aesthetics compromise communication:** Klingon's design was primarily for fiction, not communication. VELA should prioritize usability.
- **Conlang communities add life:** The Klingon Language Institute showed that a conlang can develop genuine scholarly community.

## 4. Dothraki (2009)

### 4.1 Background

**Creator:** David J. Peterson
**Type:** Fictional auxiliary language for HBO's *Game of Thrones* / George R.R. Martin's *A Song of Ice and Fire*
**Goal:** Support authentic representation of a nomadic horse warrior culture
**Date:** Developed 2009 for HBO's *Game of Thrones* Season 1; Peterson was hired after the show acquired the rights and found Martin's Dothraki vocabulary insufficient

### 4.2 Design Philosophy

Peterson's approach was **naturalistic by design** — Dothraki should feel like a natural language, not an invented one. He drew inspiration from real languages of nomadic, horse-riding cultures (particularly Turkic languages, Mongolian, and some Native American languages) for phonological patterns and grammatical features.

**Key Design Features:**

1. **Phonology inspired by Turkic:**
   - Vowel harmony (agglutable suffixes must agree in vowel quality with the root)
   - No consonant clusters in word-initial position
   - Guttural sounds
   
2. **Cultural vocabulary focus:**
   - Horse-related vocabulary is extremely rich (dozens of words for horse types, conditions, colors, gaits)
   - Words for concepts central to Dothraki life: *sith* (blood oath), *kosart* (horse-thief as supreme insult)
   - Limited abstract vocabulary — Dothraki culture emphasizes concrete experience
   
3. **Verb system:**
   - Complex verb conjugation with aspect marking
   - Evidential-like distinctions (the language reflects a culture that values direct experience)
   
4. **Word order:** SOV (subject-object-verb) with flexible scrambling

5. **Numerals:** Base-5 or vigesimal (20-based) — common in cultures with body-counting traditions (counting on fingers and toes)

### 4.3 What Works

- **Immersive naturalism:** Dothraki feels like a real language that evolved in a specific environment — viewers and readers sense the culture behind it
- **Cultural coherence:** The language reinforces and extends the cultural worldbuilding — horse vocabulary is vast, abstract terms are limited
- **Phonological attractiveness:** Despite its gutturals, Dothraki is pleasant-sounding and has good prosody
- **Documented methodology:** Peterson published *The Art of Language Invention* (2015), explaining his systematic approach

### 4.4 What Doesn't Work (Criticisms)

- **Some artificial markers:** Dothraki has some features that feel designed (e.g., extensive compounding)
- **Limited time depth:** Unlike Esperanto, Klingon, or Ithkuil, Dothraki doesn't have an implied history of language change — it has no proto-language, no attested sound changes, no dialectal variation
- **Very small community:** Not designed to be a community language — only used in fiction

### 4.5 Lessons for VELA

- **Naturalistic approach requires research** — Peterson studied real languages of relevant cultures before designing Dothraki. VELA's design should be grounded in typological research.
- **Cultural vocabulary investment is essential** — rich vocabulary in VELA's core cultural domain will make it feel authentic
- **Phonological inspiration from real languages** — borrowing patterns from relevant real languages adds naturalistic credibility
- **Consider building a proto-language history** — even a sketch of sound changes adds depth

## 5. High Valyrian (2011)

### 5.1 Background

**Creator:** David J. Peterson (same creator as Dothraki)
**Type:** Fictional auxiliary language for the Valyrian Freehold in *Game of Thrones*
**Goal:** Evoke a prestigious, ancient, scholarly language — the Latin of the fictional world
**Date:** Developed 2011 for *Game of Thrones* Season 2; Peterson had ~9 months to develop a usable language with substantial vocabulary

### 5.2 Design Philosophy

Peterson wanted High Valyrian to feel **ancient, prestigious, and refined** — the language of an empire that existed for millennia. He drew heavily from Latin, Ancient Greek, and to a lesser extent Sanskrit and Old Norse.

**Key Design Features:**

1. **Latin/Greek-inspired phonology:**
   - Long vowels (distinct from short vowels)
   - Consonant clusters (Latin-style: *-st-*, *-ct-*, *-gn-*)
   - /θ/ (theta) — rare, classical feel
   - No /pʃ/, /tʃ/ (Cherokee-influenced avoidance)
   
2. **Latin-inspired morphology:**
   - 8 noun cases: nominative, vocative, accusative, genitive, dative, locative, instrumental, causal
   - Complex verb conjugation with multiple tenses and moods
   - Subject-verb agreement
   
3. **Polite/formal register system:**
   - *Private* (inf) vs. *public* (public) speech — appropriate for Valyrian's aristocratic culture
   
4. **Diphthongs:** ae, oe, ui (like Latin)

5. **Vowel-stem and consonant-stem nouns** (like Latin first/declension vs. third declension)

### 5.3 What Works

- **Pragmatic feel:** Peterson balanced completeness with usability — there is enough grammar and vocabulary for TV scripts, but the language feels ancient and complete
- **Distinctive register:** The formal/informal distinction gives Valyrian social sophistication
- **Aesthetic beauty:** The Latin/Greek phonology creates a distinctly classical feel that audiences associate with ancient civilizations
- **Rapid development under deadline:** Peterson demonstrated that a naturalistic conlang can be created on a tight timeline with systematic methodology

### 5.4 What Doesn't Work (Criticisms)

- **Too Latin/Greek derivative:** Some linguists criticize Peterson for making High Valyrian feel like a Latin reskin rather than an independent invention
- **Limited documentation:** Fans have had to extrapolate extensively from the limited corpus
- **No proto-language:** Unlike natural Romance languages, High Valyrian has no implied Vulgar Proto-Valyrian with attested sound changes

### 5.5 Lessons for VELA

- **Reference languages are a tool, not a template** — draw inspiration but create something genuinely distinct
- **A noun case system** (even 4-6 cases) adds grammatical sophistication and elegance
- **Vowel length distinction** adds a layer of phonological complexity that feels more natural and "ancient"
- **Register systems** (formal/informal) add social realism

## 6. Toki Pona (2001)

### 6.1 Background

**Creator:** Sonja Lang (Canadian linguist, originally published as *Toki Pona: The Language of Good*)
**Type:** Philosophical minimalist language / thought experiment
**Goal:** Promote simplicity, essentialism, and positive thinking through language design
**Date:** First published 2001; revised edition 2014

### 6.2 Design Philosophy

Toki Pona is the radical opposite of languages like Ithkuil — instead of maximum information density, it pursues **minimum vocabulary with maximum conceptual coverage**. The language has only 120-139 words (depending on counting) and an extremely simple grammar.

**Key Design Features:**

1. **Radically minimal vocabulary:**
   - No colors — only *laso* (blue/green), *walo* (white/light), *pimeja* (black/dark)
   - No numbers beyond 2 (*wan*, *tu*) — larger numbers expressed through compounding or approximation
   - Each word has a broad semantic range: *lon* = be in/at/exist/real/true
   
2. **Simple grammar:**
   - SVO word order (fixed)
   - No conjugation, no declension, no gender
   - Particles indicate grammatical relationships: *li* (predicate marker), *e* (direct object marker)
   
3. **Semantic philosophy:**
   - The limited vocabulary forces speakers to focus on the essence of concepts
   - Inspired by Taoist philosophy: simplicity, naturalness, humility
   - "Think less, say more" — compression of meaning
   
4. **Compounding and derivation:**
   - Words combine to create specific meanings: *tomo* (room/house) + *suli* (big) = *tomo suli* (big house / mansion / cathedral)
   - Despite 120 words, thousands of compound meanings are possible

### 6.3 What Works

- **Extraordinary learnability:** Most people can learn Toki Pona in days
- **Philosophical appeal:** The language has attracted speakers interested in its conceptual approach
- **Active community:** Online community, original music, poetry, even a Bible translation
- **Proof of concept:** It proves that a language with 120 words is genuinely usable for communication

### 6.4 What Doesn't Work (Criticisms)

- **Loses too much information:** Without color terms, numbers, or fine-grained vocabulary, many concepts are cumbersome to express
- **English word order (SVO) is rigid:** This may reflect Anglocentrism despite the language's globalist aspirations
- **Semantic crowding:** Having one word (*lon*) mean 15+ different things creates ambiguity that must be resolved through context
- **Not suitable for complex domains:** Science, law, technology — all require cumbersome paraphrase

### 6.5 Lessons for VELA

- **Minimalism is a valid design choice, but choose the right domain for it** — Toki Pona works as a philosophical language but not as a general-purpose one
- **Even a small vocabulary can be extended through compounding** — this principle should be central to VELA's lexicon design
- **Fixed word order simplifies grammar dramatically** — a trade-off worth considering
- **The Sapir-Whorfian dimension** (language shapes thought) is a legitimate conlang motivation — VELA can be designed to encourage certain cognitive patterns

## 7. Ithkuil (1978-2004)

### 7.1 Background

**Creator:** John Quijada
**Type:** Experimental philosophical/logical language
**Goal:** Create the most semantically precise language possible — minimizing ambiguity and maximizing cognitive encoding
**Date:** Begun 1978; *Ithkuil: A Philosophical Sketch* published 2004; revised as Ithkuil il 2012

### 7.2 Design Philosophy

Quijada wanted to solve what he saw as fundamental problems in natural languages: **vagueness, redundancy, and inefficiency**. The result is a language of extraordinary complexity — arguably the most sophisticated conlang ever designed.

**Key Design Features:**

1. **Radical morphological density:**
   - Single words encode concepts that would require entire sentences in English
   - Example: The word *Trramalži* encodes: "a small, female, domestic feline that is sitting on a chair in the corner of the room, engaged in the act of sleeping"
   
2. **Extensive affixation system:**
   - Hundreds of affixes encoding semantic categories
   - Categories include: Essence (abstract/concrete), Constitution (solid/liquid/gaseous), Perspective, Affinity, Constitution, and many more
   
3. **No verbs in the traditional sense:**
   - "Verbs" are roots with slot-frames that encode case relationships
   - The language is more about states and relations than events
   
4. **Phonological system:**
   - Complex consonant clusters (up to 5 phonemes)
   - Large vowel inventory
   - Tonal distinctions
   
5. **No regular noun-verb distinction:**
   - Roots can function as either depending on the framework

### 7.3 What Works

- **Extraordinary semantic precision:** If a concept exists, Ithkuil can express it compactly
- **Cognitive coherence:** The language's categories reveal genuine insights into human cognition
- **Intellectual achievement:** It demonstrates that a single person can create a complete linguistic system rivaling natural languages in complexity
- **Active community:** Despite its difficulty, Ithkuil has devoted followers

### 7.4 What Doesn't Work (Criticisms)

- **Near-impossible learnability:** Ithkuil takes years to master — defeating the communicative purpose
- **No native speakers:** No one speaks it as a first language; it has never been used for natural conversation
- **Over-engineered:** Quijada's perfectionism has resulted in a language that exists primarily as intellectual exercise
- **Not designed for community use:** It's an art project, not a communication tool

### 7.5 Lessons for VELA

- **Aim for learnability** — no conlang has native speakers; all must be learned. An unusable language is an interesting philosophical experiment but not a living conlang
- **Semantic precision can be achieved without Ithkuil-style complexity** — natural languages do this through context, discourse, and pragmatic inference
- **Morphological density is a spectrum** — VELA should be more morphologically rich than English but far less than Ithkuil
- **Distinguish between design for beauty/design for use** — VELA should be primarily usable

## 8. Láadan (1982)

### 8.1 Background

**Creator:** Suzette Haden Elgin (American linguist and science fiction writer)
**Type:** Feminist language / social/political thought experiment
**Goal:** Test the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis by creating a language that makes women's perceptions easier to express
**Date:** Created 1982; published in Elgin's novel *Native Tongue* (1984)

### 8.2 Design Philosophy

Elgin believed that natural languages were male-centered and made it harder for women to articulate their experiences. She designed Láadan specifically to address this gap.

**Key Design Features:**

1. **Unbiased assertion markers:**
   - Every statement must be marked for the speaker's emotional relationship to the content: *-ba* (speaker feels positive about statement), *-bi* (negative), *-be* (neutral/objective)
   - This makes subjective/objective stance explicit and obligatory
   
2. **Rich emotional vocabulary:**
   - Words for experiences central to women's perspectives that English allegedly makes difficult to express
   - Example: *walthi* = "experiencing the pain of an unexpressed creative idea"
   - *zhaya* = "the feeling of not being taken seriously due to your physical appearance"
   
3. **Hearsay markers:**
   - Distinguishes between direct observation, inference, and hearsay at the grammatical level (similar to evidentiality)
   
4. **Phonological accessibility:**
   - No difficult sounds — designed to be easy for English speakers
   - Pleasant, musical sound (high frequency of vowels, liquid sounds)
   
5. **Standard grammar features:**
   - SVO order
   - No gender
   - Agglutinative morphology

### 8.3 What Works

- **Unique conceptual framework:** The language reveals assumptions about what is "objective" vs. "subjective" that are embedded in English grammar
- **Provocative philosophical agenda:** It sparked genuine debate about Sapir-Whorf, gender in language, and linguistic universalism
- **Active community:** Despite the language's small size, it has a dedicated community

### 8.4 What Doesn't Work (Criticisms)

- **Alleged bias is questionable:** Critics argue that English can express the same concepts; Elgin overstated the alleged inadequacies
- **Limited vocabulary and grammatical depth:** Láadan was designed as a novel prop and philosophical statement, not a full language
- **The Sapir-Whorf test was never properly conducted:** The novel's premise was theoretical; no controlled study was ever done

### 8.5 Lessons for VELA

- **Grammaticalizing emotional stance** is a powerful design choice — languages that mark speaker attitude are more expressive
- **Every conlang encodes a worldview** — VELA's design decisions will reflect assumptions about what is important, real, and expressible
- **Vocabulary can be culturally revealing** — invest in VELA's cultural vocabulary to make it feel authentic
- **The Sapir-Whorf test is a valid conlang motivation** — VELA could be designed to emphasize certain conceptual distinctions over others

## 10. Synthesis: What VELA Should Take From Each

**From Esperanto:**
- Regularity, systematicity, and decomposability are essential
- Correlative systems add elegance
- Avoid false universals hidden in "neutral" design

**From Klingon:**
- Sound serves culture — harsh sounds serve warrior culture
- Invest heavily in culturally central vocabulary
- Cultural coherence strengthens immersive quality

**From Dothraki:**
- Study real languages of similar cultural/ecological context
- Proto-language history adds depth
- Phonological patterns can be borrowed from relevant real languages

**From High Valyrian:**
- Classical phonology (long vowels, consonant clusters, theta) evokes age and prestige
- A noun case system adds grammatical elegance
- Register distinctions add social sophistication

**From Toki Pona:**
- Compounding extends a small vocabulary far
- Fixed word order simplifies grammar without sacrificing much
- Minimalism in grammar is achievable

**From Ithkuil:**
- Learnability must be a design constraint, not an afterthought
- Natural languages achieve precision through context, not through maximum morphological density
- Semantic precision can be grammaticalized (e.g., evidentiality)

**From Láadan:**
- Grammaticalizing speaker stance (emotional valence) adds expressiveness
- Every conlang encodes a worldview — design VELA's deliberately
- The Sapir-Whorf dimension is a legitimate design motivation
